How buying from a builder works in Utah County

I spent 3 years on the builder side with Richmond American Homes, running 5 new construction communities in Utah County and closing more than 100 homes. These are the 9 steps I want every buyer to understand before they walk into a model home.

The mistake I watched from the sales office

I once worked with buyers who wanted one specific lot. They waited a full year for it to open up. When it finally did, they came in to price out the home, and the numbers stacked up fast.

Lot premium$50,000
Third car garage$25,000
Other structural upgradesincluded below
Above base priceabout $85,000

And that was before they ever sat down at the design center.

The final price landed so far above what they'd planned that they priced themselves out of the home they had waited a year for. Almost every step below is about keeping that from happening to you, and it starts with knowing your budget before you fall in love with a floor plan.

The 9 steps of buying from a builder

  1. Find and interview buyer's agents

    Look for an agent with real new construction experience. When you buy from a builder, the builder covers your buyer's agent's commission. Brokerage fees are separate and vary from brokerage to brokerage, so ask about them up front.

    The part most people miss: bring your agent to your very first appointment with the builder. If you don't, the builder doesn't have to pay your agent, and you could be held responsible for paying them out of pocket.

  2. Get pre-approved

    Know your budget before you ever start your search. When you know the price point you're comfortable with, you only tour homes that fit it, and that saves you a lot of time.

    I always recommend talking to a mortgage broker over a bank or credit union. A bank might have 3 or 4 loan options. A broker could have 100. I have several lenders I can recommend.

  3. Choose the right builder

    Decide what you want in a home and which neighborhoods and communities you'd like to live in. Then do your own research on the builders and community pages in the areas you're most interested in.

    In South County alone I keep up with 14 builders, from townhome communities to luxury homes, so I can tell you how the ones on your list compare.

  4. Understand base price

    Base price is the number a builder advertises to get you in the door. It includes all the standard finishes. It does not include lot premiums, structural upgrades, or design center upgrades.

    The buyers in my story planned around base price. The $85,000 added on top of it is what priced them out.

  5. Choose your lot and structural options

    Lot premiums and structural changes (an extra bedroom, a larger garage, a finished basement) are decided early, and they add up fast. For the buyers who waited a year, the lot alone was $50,000 and the third car garage was another $25,000. Price these out before you commit to a floor plan.

  6. Review the purchase agreement and incentives

    Builder contracts are written by the builder. Before you sign, know these three things:

    • When your money goes hard, meaning the point where your deposit is no longer refundable.
    • How much the builder requires out of pocket for upgrades. Some builders require 20% of all upgrades. Others require a varying percentage once your upgrades pass a certain amount.
    • What incentives are available. Lately I've seen a lot of rate buydowns, anywhere from buying down a full 30-year fixed rate to temporary 3-2-1 buydowns. It's also common for builders to offer a flat dollar amount you can use however you want.

  7. Make your design center selections

    Go in with a set upgrade budget, and spend it first on what's hard to change after you move in. The order I give my buyers: floors, carpet pad, cabinets, then countertops.

    After that, extra wiring and outlets matter more than most people expect. If you need a home office, run Cat 6 to it, and sketch a basic office layout ahead of time so you know exactly where the Cat 6 and extra outlets should go.

  8. Inspections during construction

    Almost every builder allows an independent inspection, but most allow only one, and it's usually right before closing. That means the framing, plumbing, and wiring get covered by drywall before your inspector ever sees them.

    That's why you want an agent who knows what they're looking at on a pre-drywall walk.

  9. Final walkthrough, closing, and warranty

    At your final walk, get as many items as possible marked and added to the superintendent's list. Documentation is your friend with warranty claims. If you can show something needed to be fixed before closing, it's much easier to get the builder back out for it.

    After you move in, most builders do one follow-up about a year after closing unless something big comes up. They don't have the manpower to come out for every small item, so keep a running list for that visit.
